Foreign Investors Buying Shares in Vietnam: Step-by-Step Guide

The process for a foreign investor to contribute capital to or buy shares in a Vietnamese company, with key milestones and accounting tasks.

Acquiring a stake in a Vietnamese company shortens the time to market entry: customers, staff and licenses are already in place. But the procedure for a foreign investor to contribute capital or buy shares involves several consecutive steps, and getting the order wrong means the bank refuses the transfer or the amendment file is returned. This article sets out the process in the order it happens in practice, together with the accounting preparation required, based on the FDI accounting experience of ACCA Consulting Vietnam.

1. When to acquire capital or shares rather than set up a new company

Contributing capital or buying shares makes sense when the target company already holds value that is hard to recreate quickly: customer contracts, a team, premises and sector licenses. For Vietnamese business owners, it is also a way to raise capital or sell down part of their stake. If you are unsure whether to acquire or start from scratch, read comparing 5 forms of investment in Vietnam.

The biggest difference from setting up a new company: the investor inherits the entire accounting and tax history. Due diligence must therefore always come before signing the contract.

2. The 6-step process for a foreign investor to acquire capital or shares

Step 3 depends on the business line, the post-transaction ownership ratio and other factors under current regulations. Do not assume that “buying less than 50% needs no registration”; check each case.

3. Milestones to track

If, instead of buying an existing stake, the investor contributes new capital to increase charter capital, the contribution deadline under the regulations must be checked. For a newly established FDI company, capital must be fully contributed within 90 days of the issue date of the Enterprise Registration Certificate (ERC – Giấy chứng nhận đăng ký doanh nghiệp).

4. Impact on ownership structure and the books

After the transaction, the company may become a foreign-invested enterprise. The requirement to have annual financial statements audited by an independent audit firm should then be considered from the financial year in which the change occurs. The accountant also needs to update the register of members/shareholders, track contributed capital by owner and prepare for related-party transactions.

5. Common mistakes that delay the transaction

  • Transferring funds before the capital contribution registration is approved (where required), forcing the bank to return the money or leaving the transaction on hold.
  • A transfer description that doesn't match the transfer agreement, making it hard to prove the source of capital and to repatriate profits later.
  • Forgetting to declare the seller's capital transfer tax, especially when the seller is an individual or a foreign organization.
  • Not updating the books after the change of ownership, leading to errors in the financial statements and audit file.

FAQ

Does a foreign investor buying shares need an IRC?

Usually, contributing capital to or buying shares in an existing company does not follow the Investment Registration Certificate (IRC) route used for a new company, but prior registration of the capital contribution may be required depending on the business line and ownership ratio. Check your specific case.

Which account should the share purchase payment go through?

The funds must pass through the appropriate investment capital account under foreign exchange regulations. Work with the bank before transferring to confirm the right account type and transfer description.

Does the seller have to pay tax?

Tax may arise on income from the capital transfer, depending on whether the seller is an individual or an organization. The filing obligation should be identified when the contract is drawn up to avoid late-payment interest.
