FX Differences in FDI Companies: Accounting and Period-End Revaluation

Accounting for realized exchange differences and period-end revaluation, through a case involving a parent-company loan and USD supplier payables.

An FDI company that keeps its books in VND but borrows from its parent, buys materials and sells in USD will record foreign exchange differences almost every month. Mistakes here distort profit, distort group reporting and can affect the corporate income tax (CIT/TNDN) finalization. This article, prepared by the accounting team at ACCA Consulting Vietnam, walks through a case with illustrative figures so you can see each entry clearly.

1. Two types of exchange differences to distinguish

In practice, FDI accountants deal with two types of differences, each recognized at a different time:

  • Realized differences: arise when a foreign currency payable or receivable is settled at a rate different from the rate at which it was recorded. Gains go to financial income (account 515) and losses to financial expenses (account 635).
  • Revaluation differences: arise when the company revalues the balances of foreign-currency monetary items (cash, receivables, payables, loans) at the reporting date. The differences are first recorded in account 413 and then transferred under the current accounting regime.

Non-monetary items such as inventory, fixed assets and prepayments are not revalued at the period-end rate. A common mistake is revaluing advances paid to suppliers as well.

2. Illustrative case: a parent-company loan and supplier payables

Company X (hypothetical) is an FDI company keeping its books in VND, with a financial year matching the calendar year. During the year it has two main foreign currency transactions. All exchange rates below are illustrative, not actual rates.

Entry on paying the supplier

When USD 40,000 is paid, the payable is reduced at the recorded rate (25,100), i.e. VND 1,004,000,000, while the foreign currency deposit is reduced at the actual rate on payment (25,300), i.e. VND 1,012,000,000. The VND 8,000,000 difference is a realized exchange loss: Dr 331: 1,004,000,000; Dr 635: 8,000,000; Cr 1122: 1,012,000,000.

Period-end revaluation entry

At 31 December, the USD 200,000 loan principal is still outstanding. Revaluation: 200,000 × (25,400 − 25,000) = VND 80,000,000; the loan liability increases, so the company records a loss. The USD 30,000 receivable is revalued upwards by VND 4,500,000, a gain. Both amounts are accumulated in account 413, then offset, and the net difference is transferred to account 515 or 635 under the current accounting regime.

4. Impact on corporate income tax

Accounting and tax do not always treat items the same way. Realized differences related to business operations are generally included in income or expenses when determining taxable income. For differences arising from the period-end revaluation of balances, the CIT rules treat each category of item differently; the company should check the current regulations and make adjustments on the finalization return where necessary.

For a parent-company loan, there are two further issues besides the exchange rate: interest expense between related parties may be capped (see the interest expense cap), and foreign loans must comply with foreign exchange management regulations. A common mistake is recording the entire revaluation loss as a deductible expense without review.

FAQ

Is monthly revaluation mandatory?

It is mandatory at the financial reporting date under the accounting regime. Many FDI companies revalue monthly or quarterly for group reporting, which is not contrary to the regulations if applied consistently.

Are exchange losses deductible for CIT purposes?

It depends on the type. Realized differences related to business operations are generally deductible, while revaluation differences require checking the current tax rules for each item.

Are advances paid to foreign suppliers revalued?

Usually not, because they are non-monetary items: the company will receive goods or services, not cash back.

Does a company keeping its books in USD have exchange differences?

Yes, but on VND transactions such as payroll, taxes and domestic expenses. See also the article on using USD as the accounting currency.
