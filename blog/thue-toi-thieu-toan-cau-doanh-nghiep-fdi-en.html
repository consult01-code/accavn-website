How the Global Minimum Tax Affects FDI Companies in Vietnam

An explanation of Pillar Two, the domestic top-up tax and the accounting data FDI companies should prepare so they are not caught off guard.

The global minimum tax is no longer just a matter for group headquarters. Now that Vietnam applies a top-up tax under the global anti-base erosion rules, FDI subsidiaries must provide data, recalculate their effective tax rate and may incur a payment obligation in Vietnam. This article by ACCA Consulting Vietnam helps you understand the mechanism, assess your exposure and prepare your books early.

1. What is the global minimum tax (Pillar Two)?

Pillar Two (Trụ cột 2) is a set of rules developed by the OECD/G20 to ensure that large multinational groups bear a minimum effective tax rate in each jurisdiction where they operate. If the effective tax rate in a country falls below the minimum, the difference is collected as a top-up tax.

The key point: the question is not "what is the headline rate?" but "after all incentives, exemptions and reductions, how much is actually paid on profit?". A company enjoying a preferential rate or a tax holiday can have a very low effective tax rate, even though Vietnam's standard corporate income tax (CIT/TNDN) rate is 20%.

The group consolidated revenue threshold, the minimum rate and the detailed calculation are set out in separate legal documents. Companies should check the current regulations or ask the group tax function rather than relying on hearsay.

Two collection mechanisms commonly referred to

  • Qualified Domestic Minimum Top-up Tax (QDMTT): the host country (Vietnam) itself collects the top-up tax of the subsidiary located in Vietnam, instead of leaving it to another country.
  • Income Inclusion Rule (IIR): the country of the ultimate parent company collects the shortfall from subsidiaries with a low effective tax rate.
  • In practice: if Vietnam does not collect it, that tax may be collected elsewhere. Traditional tax incentives therefore no longer deliver their full benefit to in-scope groups.

2. Which FDI companies are affected by the global minimum tax?

Not every FDI company is in scope. Typically, the rules cover constituent entities of multinational groups whose consolidated revenue exceeded the prescribed threshold in a number of recent years. Small FDI companies set up by individual investors are usually out of scope, but it is still advisable to obtain written confirmation from the group.

  • The benefit of tax incentives shrinks; expected cash flows for profit repatriation may decrease.
  • New filing and data-provision obligations arise, with their own templates and deadlines, in addition to the usual CIT finalization.
  • The group may shift its interest to forms of support other than preferential tax rates.

4. Accounting data to prepare for the global minimum tax

Most of the difficulty lies in the data, not the formula. The subsidiary needs to produce figures under the accounting framework used for consolidation (usually IFRS or the parent's framework), then adjust them under the Pillar Two rules. For how to build the conversion schedule, see the reporting package sent to the parent company.

5. A 5-step preparation roadmap for FDI subsidiaries

Coordinate with the usual CIT finalization calendar: the deadline for the CIT finalization return and the annual financial statements is the last day of the third month after the financial year end, and FDI companies must have their annual financial statements audited. Top-up tax figures should be based on audited numbers to avoid repeated adjustments.

FAQ

Do small FDI companies need to worry about the global minimum tax?

Usually not, because the rules target groups with large consolidated revenue. Even so, you should obtain confirmation from the parent company for your records and to avoid misunderstandings if the tax authority asks.

Does the domestic top-up tax replace CIT?

No. CIT is still declared, provisionally paid and finalized as usual. The top-up tax is a separate obligation, calculated on the shortfall in the effective tax rate.

Will a company currently enjoying tax incentives lose them?

Incentives under Vietnamese law are still recognized, but if the group is in scope, the tax saved may be collected back through the top-up tax. A trial calculation is needed to see the actual impact.

Who in the company should be responsible for this data?

Usually the chief accountant or the finance director, working with the group tax function. An accounting service provider can help prepare the base figures from the books.
