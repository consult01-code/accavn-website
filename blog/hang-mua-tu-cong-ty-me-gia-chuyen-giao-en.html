Buying from the Parent: Transfer Pricing Risks and Safeguards

FDI distributors and contract manufacturers buying from their group: read your profit margins to spot transfer pricing risk early.

When most goods and materials are bought from the parent company, transfer pricing (giá chuyển giao) determines almost all of an FDI company's profit in Vietnam. Persistent losses while revenue keeps growing is a signal the tax authority tends to notice. This article helps you read your own profit margins, choose a suitable comparison method and keep the right data. It draws on the accounting experience of ACCA Consulting Vietnam.

1. Why do purchases from the parent company carry transfer pricing risk?

The price paid to the parent is not the result of negotiation between two independent parties. The group may set prices according to its global strategy: shifting profit to low-tax locations or to the company that owns the brand. As a result, the Vietnamese subsidiary may be left with a very thin margin, or even a loss.

Vietnamese law requires the prices of transactions with related parties to comply with the arm's length principle. If the company cannot prove this, the tax authority may reassess the price or profit, leading to back-assessed corporate income tax (CIT/TNDN) and late-payment interest of 0.03% per day on the tax paid late.

2. Reading profit margins: a hypothetical distributor case

Company B (illustrative example) imports equipment from its parent for distribution in Vietnam. Annual revenue is VND 50 billion, cost of goods sold VND 47 billion, and selling and administrative expenses VND 2.75 billion. The gross margin is only 6% and the net margin 0.5%.

An independent distributor in the same industry usually has to bear its own marketing, warehousing and sales team costs. It therefore needs a gross margin large enough to cover these costs and still make a profit. If a dataset of comparable independent companies shows clearly higher margins, the purchase price from the parent may be set too high.

Warning signs to review yourself

  • Losses for several consecutive years while revenue grows and the group remains profitable.
  • Purchase prices from the parent rise while selling prices in the market stay the same.
  • The Vietnamese company bears large marketing costs to build the group's brand without being compensated.
  • Year-end price adjustments without a contract or clear formula.
  • A contract manufacturer receives processing fees lower than the costs it actually incurs.
  • For the same item, the price paid to the parent is higher than the price paid to independent suppliers.

3. Transfer pricing methods: which one to choose?

The related party transaction regulations set out a group of methods similar to international guidance. The choice depends on the company's functions and the data available.

In practice, the comparable profits method is the most widely used for distributors and contract manufacturers because comparable data is easier to find. Whichever method you choose, you must be able to explain the reason for choosing it in the transfer pricing documentation (if you are required to prepare it).

4. How to prevent transfer pricing risk

Year-end price adjustments that reduce profit in Vietnam are usually closely scrutinized. If the group sends an additional debit note after the financial statements have been prepared, seek advice before recording it as an expense.

5. What should the accounting team prepare during the year?

Accountants do not set transfer prices themselves, but they are the first to see the numbers. A quarterly margin report to the board of directors helps the company negotiate with the group before the financial year ends, rather than having to explain itself after a tax audit.

FAQ

Will a loss-making FDI company definitely face a transfer pricing reassessment?

Not necessarily. Losses may result from the early investment phase, a market downturn or legitimate business reasons. What matters is that the company has documents explaining the cause and proving that its purchase prices comply with the arm's length principle.

Are customs value and transfer pricing related?

Yes. The same purchase price from the parent affects both the dutiable value for import duty and CIT. Adjusting prices after import may require supplementary customs declarations, so the two functions need to coordinate.

Must every FDI company prepare transfer pricing documentation?

Not in every case. Some companies are exempt from preparing documentation under current regulations, but they must still file the related party transaction appendix and comply with the arm's length principle.
